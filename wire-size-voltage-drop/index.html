How this calculator works

Voltage drop is the voltage lost in the wire itself. For DC and single-phase AC it is 2 × one-way length × current × resistance per foot; for three-phase it uses √3 instead of 2. Resistance comes from the copper values in NEC Chapter 9, Table 8, and the current rating from the 75 °C copper column of NEC Table 310.16, with the usual limits on 14, 12 and 10 AWG.

The calculator then finds the smallest copper size that keeps the drop under your target and carries the current. If you tick "continuous load" (3 hours or more), it sizes for 125% of the current, as the electrical code does for continuous loads.

What target should I use?

A common rule of thumb, found in the informational notes of the National Electrical Code, is no more than 3% on a branch circuit and 5% in total. Long runs to outbuildings often end up sized by voltage drop rather than by current.

Low-voltage DC (12, 24 and 48 V)

At 12 V the same wattage needs ten times the current it would at 120 V, so a short run can need very heavy wire. If a 12 V run comes out huge, consider a higher system voltage.

Before you dig a trench

Burial depth, conduit, aluminum vs. copper, temperature, and how many wires share a conduit all change the answer. Use this to plan and price, then have a licensed electrician confirm the design and pull any permit your county requires.
