How this calculator works

It adds up daily energy (watts × hours per day for each load), divides by the inverter's efficiency, and multiplies by the days of backup you want. Because batteries shouldn't be drained to zero, it then divides by the usable depth of discharge: about 80% for lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) and 50% for lead-acid. That gives the battery capacity in watt-hours, shown in amp-hours at your bank voltage.

The solar array has to replace one day's energy in the sun you actually get: daily watt-hours ÷ (peak sun hours × (1 − system losses)). The charge-controller estimate is array watts ÷ battery voltage, with a margin.

Finding your peak sun hours

Peak sun hours are the equivalent hours of full sun per day. They vary by season and place. Size for your worst month if the system has to work all year; NREL's free PVWatts tool gives monthly figures for any address.

Things that make you need more

Cold batteries hold less, batteries lose capacity as they age, inverters draw power even when idle, and shade on one panel can cut a whole string. Round up.

Safety

Battery banks store a lot of energy. Use proper fusing, rated cable and a listed enclosure, and have an electrician review anything connected to your house wiring.

Farm Power Math is a free planning tool. Results are estimates, not engineering or electrical advice. Check your local code and have a licensed electrician confirm anything before you install it.
